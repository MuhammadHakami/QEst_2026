The Minimum Birkhoff Decomposition (MBD) seeks a sparse weighted sum of matchings and is a
challenging optimization problem with applications in network scheduling and energy trading. We
scale a quantum-assisted decomposition method by combining single-layer QAOA sampling with
Extended Fully-Corrective Frank-Wolfe (E-FCFW) optimization, spectral graph partitioning, and
greedy feasibility repair. We demonstrate the pipeline on the 1,354-node PEGASE bus test case
(representing a 1,354-bus transmission grid with 1,710 transmission lines), whose 1,710 edges define
a native 1,710-variable matching optimization problem requiring 1,710 qubits in the unpartitioned
encoding. Partitioning enables distributed execution on IBM superconducting quantum processors.
The best reported hardware result uses a 50-qubit partition bound, while reducing partitions to 20
qubits degrades convergence in the partition-size comparison. Larger partitions are more demanding
for matrix product state (MPS) simulation, and bond-dimension tests show that truncating quantum
correlations reduces candidate quality. The results therefore motivate retaining a substantive quantum
sampling task within each partition as the overall problem scales. On the PEGASE-1354 benchmark,
repaired QAOA samples achieve lower residual decomposition error than both simulated annealing
and uniform random sampling baselines, while spatial circuit packing reduces hardware execution time
by approximately a factor of three. These results demonstrate that combining spectral partitioning,
spatial circuit packing, and classical feasibility repair provides an executable path for deploying
gate-based quantum sampling on thousand-variable constrained optimization problems on current
hardware.
I.

INTRODUCTION

Network scheduling, optical switching, and peer-to-peer
energy trading [1, 2] require weighted demands to be
served through compatible sets of connections [3, 4]. Representing each set as a matching gives a graph decomposition problem: reconstruct the demand graph with
as few weighted matchings as possible. Fewer matchings
reduce switching and reconfiguration overhead. This extends the Birkhoff–von Neumann decomposition from
bipartite graphs to general network topologies [5].
Minimum Birkhoff Decomposition (MBD) is NPhard [6] and can challenge classical optimization at small
instance sizes. Its inclusion in the Quantum Optimization
Benchmarking Library (QOBLIB) identifies it as a relevant test problem for progress toward quantum advantage
in optimization [7]. The difficulty lies in selecting a sparse
decomposition; finding an individual maximum-weight
matching remains polynomial-time solvable. Quantum
sampling is therefore considered here as a way to enrich
the candidate pool of a classical decomposition algorithm.
Building on Extended Fully-Corrective Frank-Wolfe (EFCFW) [8], we use single-layer (p = 1) QAOA to supply di-

combinations of valid matchings. We first define the MBD
as a cardinality-minimization problem over symmetric doubly substochastic matrices. We then review the Extended
Fully-Corrective Frank–Wolfe (E-FCFW) optimization
framework, which augments classical Frank–Wolfe steps
with diverse candidate sampling to achieve sparser graph
representations.

B.

Partitioning and Reconstruction

While recursive Kernighan–Lin bisection was evaluated
in preliminary benchmarking, multi-way spectral partitioning produced subgraphs with fewer cut edges and superior downstream convergence, and was therefore adopted
throughout this work. Multi-way spectral partitioning
clusters affinity-matrix eigenvectors using k-means and
recursively refines oversized clusters. Throughout, ps denotes the maximum number of edges per partition, hence
the maximum circuit qubit count, rather than a bound on
graph vertices. Spectral partitioning groups strongly connected regions while limiting the number of connections
removed from local Hamiltonians.
The residual graph is repartitioned at each iteration.
Cut edges cannot be sampled by that iteration’s local
circuits, but their residual weights can influence later
partitions. This provides a route to recovering omitted
connections, without guaranteeing that every edge becomes internal within a finite iteration budget. The global
classical maximum-weight matching oracle also remains
available to the outer solver.
Local samples are combined by batched Cartesian products or pruned beam search. We tested both methods and
used batched Cartesian products, as it maintains sample
validity more effectively. Recombination can introduce
conflicts at cut vertices shared across partition boundaries,
alongside any intra-partition collisions permitted by the
soft QUBO penalty.
To restore feasibility without introducing unselected
edges, greedy repair sorts the candidate edge set Ẽ =
{ej : xj = 1} by descending residual weight w(ej ) =
(D∗ − Dk )uv and accepts an edge if and only if both
endpoints are currently unoccupied. For |Ẽ| selected edges,
this pruning requires O(|Ẽ| log |Ẽ|) time and guarantees
matching feasibility. Because repair alters the sample
distribution without adding edges beyond those proposed
by the quantum sampler, the experiments compare QAOA
and random samples under the identical repair procedure
to isolate the contribution of sampling.

C.

Classical Update and Hardware Execution

The best distinct repaired candidates are added to
the active pool together with a global maximum-weight
matching. E-FCFW updates the nonnegative weights subject to its cardinality bound and removes zero-weight
matchings. The loop stops at the target error, a relativeimprovement plateau, or the iteration limit. Decomposition length counts retained positive-weight matchings and
need not equal the iteration index. Algorithm 1 gives the
full workflow.
Qoro’s Divi framework [18] coordinates circuit generation, sampling, reconstruction, and execution on
ibm_kingston and ibm_marrakesh. Qoro’s scheduler
packs independent circuits into disjoint regions of the
156-qubit Heron lattice, using otherwise idle capacity and
buffer qubits between subproblem boundaries to reduce
dispatch rounds (Fig. 1).

IV.

EXPERIMENTAL RESULTS

In this section, we evaluate the performance of the
distributed QAOA sampling framework on graph decomposition benchmarks. We compare partitioned E-FCFW
across varying partition bounds against classical simulated annealing and uniform random sampling baselines,
implemented both via MPS tensor network simulation
and directly on superconducting quantum processors. We
further isolate the impact of classical feasibility repair on
solution quality and demonstrate throughput speedups
achieved through multi-circuit spatial packing on an IBM
Heron device.

A.

Experimental Setup

We evaluate a synthetic Heron-topology graph with
120 vertices and 132 edges, followed by the PEGASE1354 bus test case with 1,354 vertices and 1,710 edges.
The generation of the graphs is detailed in [8]. QAOA
circuits use p = 1 and 25,000 shots per partition per
round. Simulated annealing (SA) [19] uses 1,500 reads
per partition, each with 1,000 sweeps. Uniform random
samples pass through the same stitching and repair as
QAOA and SA. These are specified sampling budgets, not
matched computational costs.
Hardware execution relied on heuristic transpilation
passes targeting minimal two-qubit gate depth. While
active error mitigation techniques—specifically dynamical decoupling using the Xp Xm pulse sequence and gate
twirling with 32 randomizations—were evaluated, empirical testing showed they degraded candidate quality on
these partitioned workloads. Consequently, hardware runs
omitted pulse-level mitigation in favor of depth-minimized
compilation coupled with downstream greedy repair (Appendix A.1).

All samplers supply the top five distinct valid candidates
to E-FCFW. The stopping criteria are normalized error
ϵ = 10−6 or relative cost improvement at most 10−3 .
Standalone greedy Birkhoff decomposition (BKF) is also
included as a baseline. Appendix A.1 gives the calibration
and stopping details.

B.

Classical Simulation and Hardware Convergence

To classically emulate and diagnose the distributed
quantum sampling pipeline, we simulate the QAOA circuits using MPS tensor networks via Maestro [20]. In this
representation, the MPS bond dimension χ bounds the
bipartite entanglement entropy across any cut by log χ,
directly controlling the amount of quantum correlation
retained in the simulated state. This allows us to systematically probe how truncating entanglement impacts
candidate sample quality and downstream decomposition
performance.
We compare final decomposition cost with mirror fidelity: the probability of recovering the all-zero state after
simulating a state-preparation circuit and its inverse at
finite χ (Appendix A.3). Higher bond dimensions improve
both mirror fidelity and final cost in Fig. 2(b). Thus, retaining the correlations lost at low χ matters for this
sampling task. This diagnostic measures the limitations
of the tested MPS approximation; it does not establish
hardness for all classical simulation methods.
Figure 2(a) compares hardware and simulated QAOA
with the classical baselines. Hardware QAOA tracks the
simulated convergence and achieves lower costs than random sampling and simulated annealing. Partitioning at
ps = 50 retains convergence close to the unpartitioned
hardware run, supporting its use to reduce circuit size without losing the useful sampling behavior in this instance.
SA makes initial progress but triggers the plateau criterion at round 18, with decomposition length 15; QAOA
continues to improve. These observations compare the
tested samplers and budgets, rather than establishing a
general separation from classical optimization.

C.

Partition Size and Circuit Density

The bounds ps ∈ {20, 50, 80} expose a trade-off between
graph fragmentation and circuit complexity. Aggressive
partitioning (ps = 20) yields uniformly shallow circuits
that are readily approximated at low MPS bond dimension
(χ ≤ 16), but convergence deteriorates as more edges are
cut. Relaxing the bound to ps = 50 and ps = 80 preserves
better convergence while reducing hardware requirements
relative to the unpartitioned problem, but circuit depth
and routing overhead become heavily dependent on local
subgraph topology rather than qubit count alone.

Because qubits represent edges rather than vertices, two
qubits interact whenever their edges share an endpoint
vertex. While the synthetic graph is generated on Heron’s
degree-3 lattice, its line graph (the interaction graph of
the edge-qubits) introduces triangles and higher local
connectivity. Subgraphs that contain dense loop structures
or clustered degree-3 junctions produce tightly coupled
line graphs that require extensive SWAP routing when
mapped back onto Heron’s physical qubits.
Conversely, partitions that form predominantly linear
paths or open trees route with minimal SWAP overhead.
Consequently, logical two-qubit depth tracks local interaction density rather than qubit count, and directly governs
the transpiled routing depth. Recent experimental demonstrations on IBM quantum computers reach two-qubit
gate depths of up to 452 with reliable output at this
qubit count [21]. However, qubit count alone is an inadequate partitioning objective: local interaction density and
hardware embedding determine execution cost. The next
scaling step should control partition density alongside
size, retaining enough interactions to challenge classical
sampling while keeping routed depth manageable.

V.

DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSION

Partitioned QAOA sampling extends E-FCFW decomposition to a 1,710-edge grid using circuits that fit current
quantum hardware. On the PEGASE-1354 bus test case,
QAOA with repair improves on random sampling with the
same repair, and spatial packing reduces execution time
by approximately a factor of three. These results establish
an executable distributed workflow for a problem class
whose classical difficulty makes it relevant to the search
for quantum advantage.
For that goal, the partitions must retain a useful quantum sampling task. Reducing them to small, easily simulated fragments would remove the reason to use a quantum

processor. The ps = 50 results provide evidence for this requirement: partitioned hardware retains the unpartitioned
convergence behavior, while the bond-dimension study
shows that aggressive truncation of quantum correlations
degrades candidate quality. This is evidence of a useful
regime, not proof of classical intractability or quantum
advantage.
Beyond partition width, the underlying topology determines where quantum sampling provides distinct utility. While extremely sparse graphs admit efficient classical solutions and dense topologies incur prohibitive
SWAP-routing overhead, intermediate-density modular
networks—such as power transmission grids—represent
the primary target regime. In these modular architectures, classical heuristics frequently become trapped in
frustrated local minima across partition cut boundaries,
whereas shallow QAOA states maintain the candidate
diversity necessary to sustain Frank-Wolfe convergence.
The next step is to target partitions of at least 100
qubits and vary their density systematically: enough connectivity to preserve useful quantum correlations, with
transpiled two-qubit depth low enough for reliable execution. Table I shows why width and density must be
optimized together.
Preliminary work towards this has been carried out on
three benchmark instances from QOBLIB’s MBD track up
to 4,096 variables [7], using partitioned QAOA executed
on IBM Heron hardware. The initial results show that the
method consistently reaches the target tolerance within

Parameter Calibration and Sampling Budgets

Offline training uses small prototypes (N ≤ 16) with
similar topology, degree distribution, and edge-weight encoding to the target family. The prototype calibration
requires approximately 100 parallel circuits and about ten
minutes of MPS simulation in the reported setup. The
resulting angles are transferred to larger circuits instead
of being optimized at each E-FCFW iteration.
For the PEGASE bus test case, the calibration landscape in Fig. 6 is evaluated on a representative 49-qubit
partition with Maestro at bond dimension 128, scanning
γ ∈ [−π, π] and β ∈ [0, π/4]. Its marked minimum near
(γ0 , β0 ) = (2.49, 0.16) supplies fixed execution angles [with
an equivalent symmetric basin near (−0.65, 0.16) due to
π-periodicity in γ]. Finite bond dimension makes this an
approximate MPS calibration; bond dimension 128 alone
does not guarantee exact simulation of a 49-qubit circuit.
SA uses a geometric cooling schedule with 1,000 sweeps
per read and 1,500 reads per partition per round, totaling 1.5 × 106 sweeps per partition. In preliminary tuning, increasing the sweep count beyond 1,000 yielded diminishing energy improvements due to thermalization
saturation, while generating 1,500 independent Markov
chain reads provided comparable serial CPU runtime to
the acquisition window of 25,000 quantum measurement
shots. Because quantum projective measurements sample

independent configurations without sequential equilibration sweeps, the shot count reflects hardware execution
throughput rather than matched algorithmic operations.
Candidate ranking and the outer stopping criteria are
shared across sampling methods.
During hardware benchmarking on ibm_kingston and
ibm_marrakesh, we evaluated dynamical decoupling [22]
with an Xp Xm pulse sequence and Pauli gate twirling
with 32 randomizations [23]. However, for shallow p = 1
QAOA sampling across distributed partitions, the additional pulse overhead and circuit duration increased effective decoherence and sampling variance without improving
the downstream repaired candidate quality. We therefore
used Qiskit’s heuristic transpilation passes (optimization
level 3) focused strictly on minimizing SWAP routing and
two-qubit depth, leaving feasibility enforcement to the
classical greedy repair routine.

Stitching and Feasibility Repair

Cartesian stitching evaluates cross-products in local
batches capped at 55 combinations per step. The alternative beam search groups subgraphs hierarchically, retains
at most 200 candidates per group, and prunes pairwise
combinations back to this limit at each merge-tree level.
Both methods limit the otherwise exponential number of
combinations.
Raw stitched candidates frequently violate matching
feasibility due to two distinct sources of collisions: (i)
intra-partition collisions, where shallow circuits and finite
QUBO penalties (λ = 0.2) permit adjacent edges to be
sampled within a partition; and (ii) boundary collisions,
where independent subproblems select edges incident on
the same shared cut vertex across partition boundaries.
Feasibility repair resolves these conflicts via a greedy
pruning pass over the candidate edge set Ẽ = {ej ∈ E :
xj = 1} extracted from the stitched bitstring:
1. The edges in Ẽ are sorted in descending order of
current residual weights w(ej ) = (D∗ − Dk )uv , with
ties broken deterministically by edge index.
2. An initially empty matching M ← ∅ and an occupied vertex set Vocc ← ∅ are initialized.
3. Each edge e = (u, v) ∈ Ẽ is evaluated in sorted
order: if u ∈
/ Vocc and v ∈
/ Vocc , the edge is accepted (M ← M ∪{e}) and its endpoints are marked
(Vocc ← Vocc ∪ {u, v}); otherwise, it is discarded.
Unselected edges (xj = 0) are never introduced, ensuring
repair acts strictly as a conflict-resolution filter on the
quantum candidate rather than an augmenting heuristic.

MPS Mirror Diagnostic

We assess MPS truncation error using Maestro’s native
mirror fidelity check (maestro.mirror_fidelity), which
implements a deterministic compute-uncompute diagnostic conceptually related to the mirror-circuit principle
[24].

approximation errors that reduce the return probability.

Orchestration and Circuit Scheduling

Divi represents residual subgraphs as MaxWeight
MatchingProblem instances within a Partitioning
ProgramEnsemble, dispatches
them
through
QoroService, and coordinates reconstruction and
repair. Qoro’s scheduler orders circuits by size and packs
them into execution rounds with a shared shot count.
It can replicate circuits with large shot requirements
on remaining qubits and adjust shot allocation to avoid
sparsely filled final rounds. Disjoint register regions
and idle buffer qubits separate packed circuits. This
reduces dispatch rounds by up to 4× in the reported
configurations; the observed PEGASE execution-time
reduction is approximately 3×.
