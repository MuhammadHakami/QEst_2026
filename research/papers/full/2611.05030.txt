On Revolutionizing Power Systems: Suitability
of Quantum Computing-Ready Pipelines for
Stochastic Unit Commitment Optimization
Abstract— Modern unit commitment (UC) scenarios,
particularly those involving uncertainties from demand
fluctuations and renewable energy sources, pose
significant challenges to traditional optimization methods
due to the large search spaces they encompass. This paper
proposes a quantum computing-ready optimization
pipeline for deterministic and stochastic UC, integrating
classical, quantum-inspired, quantum annealing, and
hybrid quantum-classical solvers across test systems,
including the IEEE 118-bus system. A novel Quadratic
Unconstrained Binary Optimization (QUBO) reformulation
accommodates generator scaling and 24-hour scheduling
horizons with compact Boolean logic penalties for startup,
cooldown, and spinning reserve constraints. A divide-andconquer strategy and Quantum Computing Monte Carlo
(QCMC) simulation address scalability across 27,730
uncertainty scenarios, reducing compilation time by up to
two orders of magnitude. Experimental results demonstrate
the pipeline's ability to achieve efficient, scalable, and
accurate UC optimization under uncertainty: the hybrid
quantum method approach matches the classical baseline,
reduces stochastic UC runtime from 40.76 to 14.79 hours,
and characterizes pure quantum annealing as limited to
small systems with 0.22% to 2.18% encoding errors and
constraint violations. These results provide an analysis of
the scalability and computational practicality of hybrid
quantum optimization for generator scheduling in UC.
Index Terms— Monte Carlo simulation, Quantum annealing,
Quantum computing, Stochastic optimization, Unit
commitment.

I. Introduction

NIT commitment (UC) optimization is a critical economic
and social practice in industrial and academic power
engineering. The UC problem is also considered an NP-hard
problem due to its combinatorial nature, which can be
expressed with either a quadratic or linearized objective
function [1]. UC objective functions are usually expressed in
their quadratic form [2], which imposes increased
computational overhead; hence, most UC optimization studies
tend to linearize objective functions [3] and grant faster
convergence. However, as shown in several readily available
solvers, this approach sacrifices information due to the piecewise sampling of the quadratic cost. Moreover, contemporary
UC optimization, both in planning and operations, accounts for
risks and uncertainties arising from demand fluctuations and
variations in renewable power generation. Two prominent
methods for addressing these challenges are robust and
stochastic UC (SUC) [4], [5] optimization methods.
Recent advancements in industrial system engineering have
sparked a growing interest in quantum computing, which has
demonstrated significant improvements in enhancing specific
problem formulations, particularly in power engineering.
Quantum computing offers numerous advantages in power
system
engineering,
including
improved
network
synchronization using qubit properties [6], enhanced
communication security through quantum key distribution [7],
and applications in large-scale optimal power flow [8], state
estimation [9], and unit commitment [10]–[12]. Given its
potential to optimize combinatorial problems, quantum
computing holds particular promise for addressing the UC
problem, where enhanced computational efficiency and
improved value estimation are critical.
Deterministic UC problems formulated as Quadratic
Unconstrained Binary Optimization (QUBO) problems can be
solved using adiabatic quantum computing. This reformulation,
however, results in an NP-complete problem due to the
combinatorial complexity of the objective function. Pure

QUBO optimization for the UC problem was demonstrated in
[13], [14], in which the UC cost is reformulated in quadratic
form, while power balance, security, and other economic
constraints are converted into penalty functions. Moreover, the
size of the UC problem, measured by the number of decision
variables and constraints, grows polynomially with the
planning horizon, the number of generating units, and the
number of discretization steps. While problem size grows
polynomially, the UC problem remains NP-hard in general due
to its combinatorial structure [1], [13]. Similar to the findings
of [15], hybrid approaches address this challenge by
decomposing the UC problem into linear or nonlinear
programming components and combinatorial subproblems,
enabling distributed optimization techniques to improve
computational efficiency [16], [17]. Despite these
advancements, significant challenges remain in retrofitting UC
for quantum systems, particularly for 24-hour UC problems that
require approximately 300 qubits, often exceeding the
capabilities of current quantum hardware. Universal quantum
computers currently provide up to 1,121 qubits for nonexclusive use, while quantum annealers offer 5,640 qubits.
Additionally, UC formulations with timing variables indicate
that PUBO exacerbates the qubit requirement when converting
to a QUBO model, implying that additional overhead qubits are
needed to accommodate continuous variable encodings and the
expansion of timing-related variables.
Beyond deterministic UC, quantum research has been
extended into distributed and stochastic settings. Nikmehr et al.
[10] extended QUBO-based UC to distributed microgrids using
quantum-enhanced alternating direction method of multipliers
(ADMM), distributing commitment decisions across classical
and quantum agents. In the stochastic domain, Rotello et al. [12]
demonstrated quantum amplitude estimation to compute
expected values in two-stage stochastic programs, thereby
establishing a quantum pathway for uncertainty-aware UC.
More recent developments have focused on Benders
decomposition-based hybrid quantum architecture. Paterakis
[18] extended this to a multi-cut Benders approach applied
directly to UC using the D-Wave Advantage quantum annealer,
exploiting multiple feasible solutions per iteration to accelerate
convergence. Hong et al. [11] further demonstrated qubitefficient quantum annealing for SUC by combining Benders
decomposition with quantum ADMM and the augmented
Lagrangian method, achieving a qubit requirement independent
of the number of Benders cuts. These decomposition strategies
represent the current frontier of hybrid quantum UC research
and directly inform the extension path of the pipeline proposed
in this work.
As shown in Table I, prior studies such as [9]–[18] have
explored QUBO reformulations for UC problems, but their
scalability evaluations are limited to small systems (e.g., fewer
than 10 generator units) and short time horizons (e.g., 6 to 12
hours). These works also omit spinning reserve constraints,
which are critical for operational feasibility in real-world
applications. Similar to [11], [12], this work presents a twostage SUC optimization model that incorporates uncertainty
variables. However, it further integrates uncertainties in both
renewable generation and demand, which is an essential factor
for a comprehensive economic assessment. Additionally, this
work evaluates the practicality of quantum optimization models

by assessing the quality of objective values obtained through
quantum annealing and hybrid quantum computing-based
optimization. To the best of the authors' knowledge, no prior
work presents a unified framework that simultaneously
addresses large-scale scalability and spinning reserve modeling
for the base UC model. The use of Quantum Computing Monte
Carlo (QCMC) simulation, which is based on quantum
amplitude estimation, represents another compelling case for a
quantum-computing-based pipeline. While prior studies such as
[12] and [18] have demonstrated practical applications, this
work focuses on exploring the potential advantages and
limitations of QCMC in the context of large-scale SUC
problems.
This paper addresses these gaps by introducing a quantumcomputing-ready SUC pipeline (see Table I) that features a
detailed QUBO reformulation incorporating compact binary
penalty terms for spinning reserves. The proposed framework
is evaluated on systems with up to 54 generators over a 24-hour
horizon and includes over 27,000 uncertainty scenarios within
a hybrid classical-quantum optimization workflow. A key
objective of this work is to characterize the practical limits of
pure quantum implementations under current hardware
constraints. In particular, pure quantum annealing is restricted
to small-scale systems and is susceptible to encoding errors
arising from qubit-constrained discretization, which tend to
increase with system size. Unlike previous quantum UC
approaches that either omit uncertainty or are constrained to
limited quantum routines, this work systematically evaluates
the suitability of quantum-inspired, annealing-based, and
hybrid solver techniques capable of handling realistic UC
constraints and large-scale data volumes, thereby analyzing the
suitability of quantum optimization including its advantages
and limitations for large-scale generator-scheduling UC
problems.
Considering the identified gaps in the existing literature, the
main contributions of this paper are described as follows:
• A quantum computing–ready optimization pipeline for
both deterministic and stochastic UC is proposed to
demonstrate its implementation across quantum-inspired,
quantum annealing, and hybrid quantum–classical solvers.
The proposed framework integrates full-scale classical
and quantum-compatible components, enabling end-toend scheduling on established UC benchmark systems,
including the IEEE 118-bus system, under a copperplate
network assumption consistent with current quantum
solver constraints.
• A novel QUBO reformulation is presented to
accommodate generator scaling and 24-hour scheduling
horizons, addressing key limitations in earlier QUBObased UC models. By incorporating compact binary
constraints via Boolean-logic penalties, the formulation
supports startup and cooldown modeling, similar to
classical Mixed-Integer Quadratic Programming (MIQP),
while remaining compatible with quantum solvers.
Additionally, a structured penalty model for spinning
reserve constraints, which is a feature often omitted in
prior quantum UC works, is integrated.
• A suitability evaluation of QUBO-based and quantumcompatible optimization methods for solving the UC

divide-and-conquer strategy for compiling large

II. PRELIMINARIES
A. Quantum Computing
Quantum computing, grounded in quantum mechanics,
offers a new paradigm for information processing by
representing and manipulating quantum states using linear
operators. Its effectiveness depends on the hardware, ranging
from digital and quantum annealers to analog and universal
quantum computers. While universal systems support advanced
models, such as adiabatic and topological computing, they are
challenging to realize. In contrast, quantum and digital
annealers optimize using Hamiltonians, with digital annealers
simulating quantum behavior on classical hardware.

III. QUANTUM COMPUTING-READY UNIT COMMITMENT
PIPELINE
Modern UC optimization requires accounting for numerous
operational constraints and uncertainty scenarios, significantly
increasing model complexity. In this study, the UC problem is
formulated as a quantum-compatible model over a 24-hour
horizon under the copperplate network assumption, whereby
transmission constraints and power flow equations are
neglected, and system-wide power balance is enforced through
a single aggregated constraint. In practical system planning,
transmission networks are typically designed to satisfy N-1 or
N-2 contingency criteria across a range of operating scenarios,
which can mitigate the likelihood of severe congestion.
However, the copperplate assumption may still lead to
infeasible schedules when transmission constraints become

binding, particularly in systems with congested corridors or
high penetration of distributed renewable generation. Ramping
constraints remain physically meaningful under this
assumption, as they capture the temporal operating limits of
individual generators independently of network topology and
line flow constraints. The omission of network constraints does
not affect the methodological development of the proposed
framework, as the primary focus of this work is on the design
of a quantum computing pipeline for SUC. Nevertheless,
incorporating network constraints represents an important
direction for future research.
To address the computational challenges of large-scale SUC,
this paper integrates QCMC techniques for sample-efficient
expectation estimation. It presents a divide-and-conquer
strategy to streamline the compilation of QUBO Hamiltonians.
As shown in Fig. 3, the proposed hybrid quantum pipeline
begins with classical preprocessing steps, such as forecasting
and uncertainty modeling, followed by UC formulation and
transformation into QUBO or Constrained Quadratic Mode
(CQM). For stochastic scenarios, the divide-and-conquer
method decomposes each scenario's QUBO model into smaller
sub-Hamiltonians, which are compiled independently and then
aggregated. This modular strategy ensures efficient compilation
while preserving constraint structure. The pipeline distributes
optimization workloads across classical and quantum
resources, leveraging central processing units (CPU), graphics
processing units (GPU), and Quantum Processing Units (QPU)
to solve deterministic and stochastic UC problems efficiently.
This synergistic integration of classical and quantum methods
enables scalable, uncertainty-aware UC optimization for
generator-scheduling problems, forming a modular foundation
extensible to network-constrained settings via Benders
decomposition.

selection ensures adequate marginal coverage for each

assumption may not hold in practice. Weather-driven
correlations such as positive coupling between solar irradiance
and cooling demand, or seasonal wind-demand dependencies
can produce joint tail events that are underrepresented in an
independent sampling scheme. In such cases, joint distribution
would exhibit heavier tails than the product of marginals,
potentially requiring a larger scenario set or correlated sampling
approaches such as copula-based methods for adequate tail
coverage. Incorporating correlation structures into the scenario
generation framework is identified as a direction for future
work, particularly for systems with strong weather-driven
renewable-demand coupling.
C. Stochastic Optimization Routine with QCMC
The hybrid CQM solver performs a structural separation of
the UC problem formulated in (7), rather than relying on
iterative decomposition. By classifying terms in 𝐻𝑄𝑈𝐵𝑂 by type,
the CQM framework routes linear objectives and linear
constraints from (9b), involving only binary commitment
variables 𝑥̃, to classical hardware (CPU/GPU), while quadratic
terms in (9a) and (9c), involving continuous power and reserve
encodings 𝜋̃, are formulated as QUBO subproblems submitted
to the QPU [23]. This single-pass partitioning based on term
structure eliminates the need for iterative subproblem exchange
and convergence criterion, distinguishing it from
decomposition-based methods such as Benders or ADMM.
Optimality is governed by the classical branch-and-bound
applied to the binary commitment variables, with QPU-derived
samples providing guidance rather than solely determining the
final solution. The modified power balance penalty 𝒫1∗ (𝑢) for

D. Divide-and-Conquer Strategy for SUC QUBO Model
Recombination
The compilation time of QUBO models scales with the number
of binary variables and their interactions, with most time
attributed to compiling the objective Hamiltonian 𝐻𝑜𝑏𝑗 . To
address this, QUBO Hamiltonians are decomposed into subHamiltonians, allowing for the separate compilation of
penalties related to uncertainty. The proposed divide-andconquer algorithm addresses the scalability challenge of
QUBO-based SUC by structuring the solution process into
three key phases: divide, conquer, and recombination. The
algorithm decomposes the high-dimensional stochastic
problem into scenario-specific QUBO subproblems, solves
each independently, and then aggregates the results to estimate
the expected system cost efficiently:
Divide Phase. In this phase, the algorithm partitions the SUC
problem into independent QUBO sub-Hamiltonians, each
corresponding to a distinct uncertainty scenario. This
decomposition reduces memory overhead, shortens
compilation time, and ensures compatibility with fixed-size
QUBO solvers, all while preserving the essential structure of
the UC problem. Each scenario-specific QUBO encodes the UC
logic and spinning reserve penalties based on its associated
forecast profile, using a consistent base penalty structure
adapted to scenario-specific parameters. Penalty terms not
related to the power balance constraint 𝒫 ∗ are compiled to form
the base Hamiltonian 𝐻 ∗ .
Conquer (Recombination) Phase. In the conquer phase, the
algorithm aggregates sub-Hamiltonians by estimating the
expectation cost using classical Monte Carlo or QCMC
simulation. Rather than merging QUBOs into a single large
model, the approach aggregates solution statistics after
independent optimizations, thereby avoiding exponential
growth in computational complexity. The strategy
approximates the full stochastic problem without requiring
simultaneous optimization over the entire scenario space,
thereby maintaining tractability while preserving fidelity in cost
estimation. For each uncertainty sample 𝓊 , which includes
demand fluctuations, wind power generation, and solar PV

power generation, the power balance penalty (15a) is compiled
as a sub-Hamiltonian 𝐻𝓊′ . New Hamiltonians incorporating
uncertainty are constructed as ℋ , derived from the sum of the
base Hamiltonian 𝐻 ∗ and the sub-Hamiltonian 𝐻𝓊′ . The
proposed divide-and-conquer method is implemented as a noniterative batch process, enabling modular compilation and
scalable optimization of QUBO Hamiltonians across large
uncertainty sets. The complete set of Hamiltonians for all

IV. DETERMINISTIC UNIT COMMITMENT SIMULATION RESULT
This section presents numerical results for deterministic UC
using multiple model formulations and optimization strategies
implemented within the quantum computing-ready pipeline
illustrated in Fig. 3. The classical approach solves the UC
problem in its MIQP form using Gurobi 11.0.3 on a CPU-based
workstation (Intel i7-11700, 11th Gen, 32 GB RAM). The
quantum-inspired approach reformulates the problem as a
QUBO model using PyQUBO 1.4.0 in Python and applies
simulated annealing with 1,240 reads on the same hardware
platform. In contrast, the hybrid approach encodes the UC
problem as a constrained quadratic model (CQM) using DWave Ocean SDK 6.x, which supports MIQP formulations.
Optimization is performed via a hybrid framework that
leverages CPUs, GPUs, and QPUs on the D-Wave Advantage
4.1 system, with a time limit of 5,000 ms. For QUBO-based
quantum annealing, the Embedding Composite sampler is
employed with a chain strength of 12,000 and up to 1,240
samples. QCMC circuits are simulated using PennyLane 0.38.0

with a CPU-based statevector backend and 4,096 shots.
Scenario generation is performed using NumPy 1.26.4, yielding
27,730 independent samples, as determined by (14a). It is noted
that all reported optimization times exclude embedding time for
quantum annealing and queue latency associated with the
hybrid CQM solver.
Scalability is evaluated across three benchmark systems: the
IEEE 6-bus system [25] (3 generators, 6-hour horizon), an 11generator system [27] (24-hour horizon), and the IEEE 118-bus
system [28] (54 generators, 24-hour horizon). The IEEE 118bus system represents a portion of the 1960s American Electric
Power System, featuring 54 generators, 118 buses, and 186
transmission lines, with smaller benchmarks used for validation
before scaling. Scenario generation uses a fixed random seed of
333 throughout all experiments to ensure reproducibility, with
uncertainty modeled via the Beta, Weibull, and normal
distributions as defined in (11)–(13). The implementation code
will be made publicly available upon publication.
A. Objective Values and Optimization Performance
Objective values and generation schedules are evaluated to
assess the accuracy of hybrid quantum-classical UC
optimization. Table II compares classical, quantum-inspired,
and quantum methods using pure and hybrid solvers, using the
classical MIQP formulation as the baseline. The quantuminspired approach, which applies simulated annealing to QUBO
models on a CPU workstation, yields objective values of
$44,719.80, $682,089.37, and $1,893,060.46 for the IEEE 6bus, 11-generator, and 118-bus systems, respectively.
Furthermore, the Quantum Annealing (pure quantum method)
can only solve the IEEE 6-bus problem and was unable to
converge for the 11-generator and IEEE 118-bus system.
The results in Table II underscore the limitations of using
pure QUBO models for both classical and quantum-inspired
setups. The quantum-inspired method, derived from a fourthorder PUBO reformulated into QUBO, suffers from
decomposition overhead and exhibits sensitivity to externally
tuned Lagrange multipliers. As system size increases, errors
grow due to a reduction in complexity and the loss of constraint
fidelity during PUBO-to-QUBO conversion. Table III further
reveals that QUBO models executed on quantum annealers
exhibit constraint violations, particularly in ramping limits,
despite being labeled feasible, highlighting the challenge of
encoding hard constraints via penalties alone in noisy quantum
environments. In contrast, the hybrid quantum approach
consistently matches the classical baseline across all systems
and produces generation schedules comparable to classical
solutions (Fig. 5). This is achieved by combining QUBO and
MIQP formulations within a CQM framework and leveraging
CPUs, GPUs, and QPUs. Constraint accuracy is ensured using
Lagrange multipliers computed via D-Wave’s Hybrid Quantum
Solver, effectively resolving the limitations seen in quantuminspired methods [23]. The limitations of the classical and
purely QUBO models, as well as the advantages of the hybrid
solver, are similar to the findings of [15].

The ramping constraint violations observed for quantum
annealing in Table IV stem from two distinct sources:
deterministic coefficient truncation, where coupling values are
clamped to D-Wave's finite precision range causing small
constraint penalties to be rounded away, and stochastic
environmental noise from thermal fluctuations and integrated
control errors. The chain strength of 12,000 and 1,240 reads
suppresses the stochastic component; the residual violations are
therefore attributable primarily to coefficient truncation, which
persists regardless of sample count. Potential mitigation
strategies include spin-reversal transforms, penalty weight
rescaling, and flux bias offset corrections. The hybrid CQM
solver avoids these issues entirely by handling ramping
constraints as linear inequalities on classical hardware,
explaining its consistent constraint satisfaction across all

The Lagrange multipliers 𝜆𝑘 are selected such that the
penalty energy associated with any constraint violation exceeds
the maximum possible reduction in objective value achievable
by any single binary variable assignment which is a standard
sufficiency condition for penalty-based QUBO formulations

the maximum startup or shutdown cost differential across
generators, and 𝜆𝑐 by the maximum spinning reserve cost
contribution. Values satisfying these bounds ensure that the
QUBO energy landscape correctly prioritizes constraint
satisfaction over objective minimization.

V. CLASSICAL SUC SIMULATION RESULTS
This section presents simulation results for the stochastic
quantum-based UC optimization pipeline, comprising a
deterministic UC baseline, uncertainty set generation,
stochastic optimization, and cost expectation estimation.
Performance is assessed in terms of objective quality and
runtime. Cost expectations are computed using classical Monte
Carlo and QCMC methods, with quantum circuits modeled and
executed via the Pennylane Python package. The analysis also
evaluates estimation accuracy relative to sample size and
reports performance improvements from the proposed divideand-conquer algorithm. Notably, the Hamiltonian formulation
is carried out classically on a CPU, deterministic optimization
is performed using either Gurobi (CPU-based) or D-Wave
(quantum annealer), and QCMC sampling is simulated on a
CPU using PennyLane.
A. SUC Classical Result Analysis
The quality of the baseline SUC solution is evaluated in
terms of its cost distribution and robustness across 27,730
uncertainty scenarios. Each scenario represents a distinct
realization of demand, wind speed, and solar irradiance, and the
resulting dispatch cost 𝐹𝑢 characterizes how well the nonanticipative commitment schedule performs under each
uncertainty realization.
Four complementary metrics are reported. The expected
cost ⟨𝐹 ∗ ⟩ and standard deviation 𝜎(𝐹 ∗ ) describe the central
tendency and spread of the cost distribution. Value at Risk
(VaR) and Conditional Value at Risk (CVaR) at the 95th
percentile quantify tail exposure. The VaR marks the cost
threshold exceeded by only 5% of scenarios, while CVaR
captures the mean cost within that worst-5% tail. Regret,
defined as the cost difference between each scenario outcome
and the best-case cost, measures the suboptimality introduced
by fixing commitment decisions before uncertainty is revealed.
Finally, the coefficient of variation 𝐶𝑉 = 𝜎(𝐹 ∗ )/⟨𝐹 ∗ ⟩ is
computed across demand and renewable penetration quartiles
to assess whether schedule performance degrades under
specific operating conditions. A robust schedule is
characterized by a low CV across all quartiles and a CVaR that
remains close to the mean, indicating stable performance under
both typical and adverse scenario combinations.

C. Classical MC and QCMC Simulations Cost Estimate
Expectation cost for stochastic quantum UC can be estimated
using either classical MC or QCMC methods. In both cases,
estimation bins define cost intervals, with larger bin counts
representing finer resolution across the search space. To align
with QCMC’s qubit structure, bins are expressed as powers of
two, where the number of qubits corresponds to ⌊log 2 (|𝒰|)⌋,
ensuring compatibility with the uncertainty set’s cardinality
|𝒰| . The estimation bins discretize the UC cost range
[min 𝐹 ∗ , max 𝐹 ∗ ] into 2𝑘 equal-width intervals, where 𝑘
denotes the number of estimation qubits. Each scenario cost
𝐹𝑢∗ is assigned to its corresponding interval, forming the

empirical distribution prob(𝐹 ∗ ) encoded into unitary 𝐴 in
(16d). Bin count introduces a bias-variance tradeoff: too few
bins produce coarse resolution and high bias, while too many
bins spread scenarios thinly across intervals increasing
estimation noise.
Table VII and Fig. 11 compare classical MC and QCMC
estimates for a sample size of 27,730. QCMC achieves its most
accurate result of $1,823,982.87 with a 0.60% error using 8
estimation bins. In contrast, classical MC attains an error of
0.05% (estimate: $1,805,658.23) but requires 128 bins.

This subsection evaluates the efficiency of the proposed

approximately 𝑡𝑐 = 11,238 seconds, with each uncertainty
scenario corresponding to a distinct QUBO formulation.
Consequently, the total compilation time is expected to scale
linearly with the number of scenarios, given by |𝒰| × 𝑡𝑐 . Table

Hamiltonian compilation approach in which each full model is

generators), the 11-generator test case, and the IEEE 118-bus

medium-, and large-scale UC scenarios, respectively. Due to

and 1,030 seconds, respectively. These results reflect a near

VI. CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORKS
This paper presents a quantum computing-ready pipeline for
solving both deterministic and stochastic Unit Commitment
(UC) problems in large-scale power systems. (a) The pipeline
integrates classical UC modeling with quantum-compatible
solvers, including simulated gate-based computing and
quantum annealing, offering a unified framework for evaluating
the practical advantages and limitations of quantum
optimization in UC scheduling. (b) It introduces a novel QUBO
reformulation that incorporates Boolean logic-based binary
penalties to model startup, cooldown, and spinning reserve
constraints, enabling scalability across both temporal and
generator dimensions. (c) To address the complexity of

XXX et al.: Revolutionizing Power Systems: A Quantum Computing-Ready Pipeline for Stochastic Unit Commitment Optimization

stochastic UC, the study applies a divide-and-conquer strategy
for efficient QUBO compilation and employs Quantum
Computing Monte Carlo (QCMC) to perform sample-efficient
cost estimation across uncertainty scenarios. Collectively, these
contributions enhance hybrid quantum optimization approaches
in power systems by improving scalability, constraint
representation, and computational efficiency.
Experimental results validate the effectiveness of the
proposed optimization pipeline for modern UC applications. In
the deterministic UC setting, the pipeline consistently matches
the optimal solutions produced by classical MIQP solvers,
achieving a stable runtime of approximately 5,000 milliseconds
across varying system sizes. This performance surpasses both
classical heuristic approaches and quantum-inspired methods.
In the stochastic case, the pipeline attains the same expected
cost as the classical benchmark on the IEEE 118-bus system
while reducing the runtime significantly from 40.76 to 14.79
hours. Quantum-inspired solvers were found to be impractical
at scale due to excessive computation times. By integrating
QCMC with a divide-and-conquer strategy, the pipeline enables
efficient cost estimation and QUBO compilation across
thousands of uncertainty scenarios, thereby demonstrating a
realistic, quantum-ready implementation of SUC optimization,
with network-constrained extensions identified as a direct path
toward real-world deployment.
Looking ahead, the projected performance gains depend on
two key hardware trajectories: qubit count scaling and hybrid
solver scaling. For qubit scaling, current D-Wave Advantage
hardware supports up to 13 generators at 𝑁=𝑀=4 over a 24hour horizon; this capacity is projected to increase to
approximately 24 and 245 generators at 10,000 and 100,000
qubits, respectively. For hybrid solver scaling, projections
indicate potential runtime improvements on the order of 100×
to 1000× over classical MIQP at system sizes exceeding 200
generators, based on the observed exponential scaling of
classical methods. These projected advantages remain
contingent upon the availability of utility-scale or fault-tolerant
quantum hardware. The results also indicate that pure quantum
annealing is currently limited to small-scale systems. Encoding
errors arising from discretization manifest as objective
deviations ranging from 0.22% to 2.18%, along with ramping
constraint violations. This limitation represents a fundamental
challenge that may be mitigated through increased qubit
capacity or hybrid decomposition strategies that offload
continuous variables to classical solvers.
Future work may explore several directions, including the
development of noise-resilient quantum hardware, industrial
integration of quantum optimization frameworks, the
application of quantum machine learning and robust
optimization for rolling UC, and the incorporation of DC power
flow constraints through Benders decomposition. In such a
framework, the quantum pipeline can address the generator
commitment master problem, while classical solvers handle the
network feasibility subproblems. For distributed multi-area
systems, the alternating ADMM provides a complementary
extension path. These directions may further advance hybrid
quantum approaches for SUC toward practical and scalable
deployment.
